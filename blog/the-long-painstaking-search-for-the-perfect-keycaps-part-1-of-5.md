All sketches of Zotter, from ideational to engineering, have it wearing Cherry keycaps. It was a simple decision: Cherry is widely available, cheap, fast, reliable and easy to get used to. I have always typed on Cherry, and I’ve found it the fastest, most ergonomic and least error-prone keycap profile for a traditional setup like Zotter53’s 40% layout with a macro row. Lately though I’ve been wondering whether Cherry keycaps were designed to solve a problem that doesn’t exist for me. And did the standardisation of keycaps in the 1980s, around the ‘+’ shaped MX stem, prematurely close off other, more democratic approaches to keycap design? I’m thinking particularly of approaches that might let us innovate at a small scale, at home, with casting or resin 3D printing. The keycap is, after all, the *one* surface of any device that we touch the most. It is also the surface that undergoes the most stress (and, if you’re into ABS plastics like me, goes shiny). Perhaps a little Cherry history wouldn’t go amiss.

![](/assets/images/cherry.png)

Cherry's gold rush was the 1980s, the decade in which terminal computers and electronic word processors were becoming smaller and more portable (coming to be known as 'luggables'—a word I have often wondered might apply to Zotter). These still had tall sculpted keycaps with bowl-shaped depressions into which the fingertips could settle, imitating—as with the staggered layout of their keys—mechanical typewriters. I bought an old Olympia travelling typewriter when living in Delhi and do still fondly remember the distinct tactile joy of dropping the weight of my fingers into the smooth scoops of the keycaps. Since a typewriter key does not have a fixed actuation point and works more like the lever of a piano hammer, one could see-saw on the keys until sure of the word to write. It was a type of fidgeting! And its own particular kind of joy! Like spinning a pen between fingers? I don't think we have a name for these things and what they bring to the embodied pleasure of writing.

These joys of course disappeared when keycap profiles (and indeed switches) began to be built for long hours doing rapid transcription and data entry. To some extent the mechanical keyboard community has tried to keep some of these pleasures of typing alive—focusing on ASMR and acoustics—and admirably so, given the formidable manufacturing constraints imposed by dated keyboard standards. The keycap profile, the switch, the stabiliser ('stabs') remain mostly outside the scope of hobbyists. Their existing design and seating mechanism means that they have to be manufactured with tolerances below 0.05mm, which cannot be achieved using 3D printing, casting, or home CNC milling. Recent entrants into the keycap profile gameplay (like the brilliant [MT3 profile](https://matt3o.com/mt3-keycap-profile-a-brief-history/) by matt3o) have only been due to commissions from bigger companies like Keychron and Mode. There are very few keycap profiles currently in circulation, and I do think more of us should think of this as a dire thing for keyboards.

A short detour through the history of Cherry probably explains how the keyboard economy ended up like this (for a longer tour, try this [Tedium](https://tedium.co/2018/07/19/cherry-mx-keyboard-history/) article). We associate the Cherry profile with legendary keyboards like the G80 of 1987. It is short and sculpted, somewhat flat, with variable row heights and angles that mimic the natural keywell formed by the typing fingers. Yet when Cherry debuted the profile in 1981 (the company was itself founded in the US in the 1950s), there were already other ways of achieving the concave typing surface we love so much. The IBM Model F, for instance, used tall keycaps of uniform height mounted on a curved steel plate, so that the keyboard formed a shallow concave dish. It is remarkable that they did this before flex PCBs made of thin polyimide film (the kind we see in laptops today) existed. They bent a very thin fibreglass PCB into shape using a hydraulic press, which, I can imagine was quite costly.

Likely high rate of production failures with bending PCBs must have also been becoming a nuisance then, as Cherry was certainly not the first keycap company to build the concave contour into the geometry of the keycaps itself. There are patents galore from the 70s; Comptec (now Signature Plastics) was probably the first commercially successful company to debut row-sculpted keycaps, releasing the high profile SA keycaps in the mid-1970s. Cherry's success was in part due to their cruciform '+' shaped MX stem design, which miniaturised and nearly totally replaced other switch designs like the beam-spring used in the IBM model F or the Mitsumi direct plunger style design used by the Commodore64.

![](/assets/images/mx-patent-84.png)

Cherry also benefited from the fact that the curvature of the keyboard became, directly or indirectly, a regulatory concern in many countries. As [Telcontar](https://telcontar.net/KBK/tech/DIN_ergonomics?utm_source=chatgpt.com) notes, German workplace ergonomic standards (Cherry moved from US to Germany in the late 70s) drove the design of many keyboards in the 1980s, when there were legal prohibitions against the height of middle-row keycaps, which had to be less than 30mm when measured from the desk surface. Anyone who has used Cherry will know the relatively large difference in heights between its upper and middle rows (the upper row is almost high profile while the middle row is practically a thin plastic wafer). I personally dislike how shallow the middle row gets, which also makes it sound different from the upper rows. But building the curvature into a relatively low, compact profile meant that keyboard's electronics and chassis could be manufactured flat whilst fulfilling compliance requirements around worker ergonomics, an opportunity that Cherry seized on, making them today—even 60 years later—the most popular keycap profiles in the mechanical keyboard world. It is sad to see, now that the patents are expired, that the company is practically [on its knees.](https://www.tomshardware.com/peripherals/keyboards/iconic-mechanical-keyboard-switch-maker-cherry-is-in-deep-financial-trouble-the-company-is-considering-selling-its-peripherals-division-to-stay-afloat)

Row-sculpting keycaps over sculpting the geometry of the keyboard itself however transfers the burden of ergonomics from the chassis, which can be built homebrew style, to something that has to be manufactured at scale using injection moulding. There are keys of different widths (measured in units, or u) in different rows, which each need separate industrial tooling to manufacture: spacebar is 6.25u in R4 (space row), shift key is 3u in R3, and so on. A second problem is created by the MX '+' stem, which is shrouded by the keycap skirt, meaning the stem of the keycap and the skirt cannot be placed on the same plane. As FDM (Fusion Deposit Modeling, the technique most common) 3D printers cannot print mid-air, the option was to either make keycaps with very short skirts, or, as Craig Andrews [suggests](https://candrews.integralblue.com/2024/03/3d-printing-high-quality-keycaps/), to try to print them at a slanted angle, which covered them in some support scars and layer lines.

When designing Zotter, a device so obsessed with haptic pleasure in typewriting, I set out to find keycaps designed around a different ethos than productivity and long hours. I wanted pleasure! Zotter is being designed for writers, not data-entry workers, and creative writing can’t be cognitively sustained for more than an hour at a time anyway. Ergonomics and pleasure are related, sure, but I want to ask: what keycaps would we have if we took a pleasure-first approach? I’m also realising there are other things I wish for in Zotter’s keycaps: a taller profile for that pianistic see-saw fidgeting, darker keycaps with lighter legends (as on the Commodore64), and acoustics that soothe the user. There’s only so much I can do with Cherry. Its primary advantage, that blank keycaps are easy to find and legend-print, disappears with darker plastic, since you can’t dye-sublimate a lighter legend onto a darker keycap. I also want the concave keywell, which rules out every profile that isn’t row-sculpted, though perhaps there are ways to sculpt the plate instead?

And so I am lost and stranded, searching for the perfect keycaps for Zotter. The search will involve plenty of discoursing on the history of computer peripherals, on changing designs from below, and on understanding where the objects we take for granted come from. Stay tuned!
