All sketches of Zotter—from ideational to engineering—have it wearing Cherry keycaps. It was a simple decision: Cherry is widely available, easy to get used to, fast, reliable, cheap. I had always used Cherry and found them to be the fastest, most ergonomic, and least error-prone keycap profile for a traditional setup. Perhaps a little bit of Cherry history won't go amiss, because I've lately been thinking whether Cherry keycaps were designed to solve a problem that does not exist for me? And also if the standardization of keycaps in the 1980s with the advent of the '+' MX stem prematurely narrowed the possibilities of other more democratic approaches to keycap designs? Particularly ones that may allow us to innovate at smaller scales, such as at home using casting or resin 3D printing? It is, after all, the *one* surface of any device that we touch the most, not to mention the one that undergoes the most amount of stress (and if you're into ABS plastics like me—shine).

Cherry's gold rush was the 1980s, the decade in which terminal computers and electronic word processors were becoming smaller and more portable (coming to be known as 'luggables'—a word I have often wondered might apply to Zotter). These still had tall sculpted keycaps with bowl-shaped depressions into which the fingertips could settle, imitating—as with the staggered layout of their keys—mechanical typewriters. I bought an old Olympia travelling typewriter when living in Delhi and do still fondly remember the distinct tactile joy of dropping the weight of my fingers into the smooth scoops of the keycaps. Since a typewriter key does not have a fixed actuation point and works more like the lever of a piano hammer, one could see-saw on the keys until sure of the word to write. It was a type of fidgeting! And its own particular kind of joy! Like spinning a pen between fingers? I don't think we have a name for these things and what they bring to the embodied pleasure of writing.

These joys of course disappeared when keycap profiles (and indeed switches) began to be built for long hours doing rapid transcription and data entry, though to some extent the mechanical keyboard community has tried to keep it alive, focusing on ASMR and acoustics, and admirably so, given the extreme manufacturing constraints imposed by dated keyboard standards. Yes, the keycap profile, the switch, the stabiliser ('stabs') remain mostly outside the scope of hobbyists. Their design means that they have to be manufactured with tolerances below 0.05mm which cannot be achieved using 3D printing, casting, or home CNC milling. Recent entrants into the keycap profile gameplay (like the brilliant [MT3 profile](https://matt3o.com/mt3-keycap-profile-a-brief-history/) by matt3o) have only been because companies like Keychron and Mode commissioned their designers to do a new keycap profile for them. Limited production runs and patents mean that these do not enter the regular market, by which I mean blanks being printed and sold online.

A short detour of the history of Cherry probably encapsulates what made the keyboard economy like this (for a longer tour, consider this [Tedium](https://tedium.co/2018/07/19/cherry-mx-keyboard-history/) article). We associate Cherry keycap profile—short, sculpted, somewhat flat but with variable row heights and angles to mimic the natural keywell formed by the typing finger—with legendary keyboards like the G80 in 1987. Yet in 1981, when it was debuted by the Cherry Input company (itself founded in the 1950s in the US) there had already existed other ways of achieving the concave sculpted typing surface that we love about Cherry so much. The IBM Model F, for instance, used tall keycaps of uniform height mounted on a curved steel assembly plate, so that the keyboard formed a shallow concave typing dish. It is in itself remarkable that they did this all before the existence of flex PCBs made out of thin polyamide film (the kind we see in laptops today), just by bending a very thin fiberglass PCB into a curved shape using a hydraulic press.

I can imagine that the cost of bending the fibreglass PCB (as well as likely high rates of production failures) must have been becoming a nuisance then, as Cherry was certainly not the first keycap company to build the concave contour into the geometry of the keycaps itself. There are patents galore from the 70s; Comptec (now Signature Plastics) was probably the first commercially successful company to debut row-sculpted keycaps, releasing the high profile SA keycaps in the mid-1970s. Cherry's success was in part due to their '+' shaped MX stem design, which miniaturised and replaced other switch designs like the beam-spring used in the IBM model F or the Mitsumi direct plunger style design used by the Commodore64.

![](/assets/images/mx-patent-84.png)

Cherry also benefited from the fact that the curvature of the keyboard became, directly or indirectly, a regulatory concern in many countries. As [Telcontar](https://telcontar.net/KBK/tech/DIN_ergonomics?utm_source=chatgpt.com) notes, German workplace ergonomic standards (Cherry moved from US to Germany in the late 70s) drove the design of many keyboards in the 1980s, when there were legal prohibitions against the height of middle-row keycaps, which had to be less than 30mm when measured from the desk surface. Anyone who has used Cherry will know the relatively large difference in heights between its upper and middle rows; the upper row is almost high profile while the middle row is practically a thin plastic wafer. I personally dislike how shallow the middle-row gets on a Cherry, making it acoustically different from the upper rows too. But building the curvature into a relatively low, compact profile meant that keyboard's electronics and chassis could be manufactured flat whilst fulfilling compliance requirements around worker ergonomics, an opportunity that Cherry seized on, making them today—even 60 years later—the most popular keycap profiles in the mechanical keyboard world. It is sad to see, now that the patents are expired, that the company is practically [on its knees.](https://www.tomshardware.com/peripherals/keyboards/iconic-mechanical-keyboard-switch-maker-cherry-is-in-deep-financial-trouble-the-company-is-considering-selling-its-peripherals-division-to-stay-afloat)

Yet, there are reasons to dislike Cherry, among other big keycap manufacturers, if you are, like me, a homebrew computer maker. Row-sculpting keycaps over sculpting the geometry of the keyboard itself transfers the burden of ergonomics from the chassis, which can be built at home, to something that had to be manufactured at scale using injection moulding, thus requiring capital to do with expensive tooling and MOQs into thousands of units. There are keys of different widths (measured in units, or u) in different rows, which each need separate tooling: spacebar is 6.25u in R4 (space row), shift key is 3u in R3, and so on. Another design decision that took the stem took moulding keycaps out of the realm of the hobbyist 3D printer or caster and into the hands of Chinese factories: the MX '+' stem was shrouded over by the keycap skirt, meaning they could not be placed on the same plane. As FDM (Fusion Deposit Modeling, the technique most common) 3D printers cannot print mid-air, the option was to either make keycaps with very short skirts, or, as Craig Andrews [suggests](https://candrews.integralblue.com/2024/03/3d-printing-high-quality-keycaps/), to try to print them at a slanted angle, which however covers them in some support scars and layer lines.

When designing Zotter—a device so obsessed with creating haptic pleasure in typewriting—I set out on a search for keycaps designed around a different ethos than productivity and long hours alone. I wanted pleasure. Zotter is a product being designed for writers not data-entry people, and creative writing is not something that can anyway be cognitively sustained for more than an hour at a time. Ergonomics and pleasure are related, sure, but I want to ask: what keycaps would we have got if we took a pleasure-first approach. I am also realising that there are other things that I wish for Zotter's keycaps: a taller profile for the pianistic see-saw fidgeting, a darker-coloured keycaps with lighter legends (as in the Commodore64), acoustics that soothe the user. There's only so much I can do with Cherry keycaps. It's primary advantage, which is that blank keycaps are easily available to be legend-printed, is also gone with the darker colour, since you can't dye-sublimate lighter legend over a darker plastic. I do also want the concave keywell, so all keycap profiles that are not row-sculpted are out of question, though perhaps there are ways to sculpt the plate? And so I am, lost and stranded, and on a search for the perfect keycaps for Zotter, which will involve plenty of discoursing on the history of computer peripherals, on changing designs from below, on understanding where objects that we take for granted come from. Stay tuned!
